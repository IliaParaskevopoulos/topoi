Pricing and packaging

Gabor-Granger

Ask buyers whether they would buy at a series of prices, then find the price that earns the most revenue.

André Gabor and Clive Granger, 1960s

  1. Choose five to eight realistic prices around your expected range.

    Tessera tests CHF 300 to 900 per plant per month.

  2. Ask each buyer whether they would buy at a given price, moving up or down depending on the answer. Plot the share who would buy.

    At CHF 400, 80% would buy; at CHF 700, 15%.

  3. Multiply each price by the share who would buy to get relative revenue.

    Revenue rises until about CHF 440, then falls quickly.

  4. The top of the revenue curve is the revenue-maximising price. Check it against costs, competitors and positioning.

    Peak at about CHF 440. Tessera sets CHF 430 for its main plan.

The question it answers

At what price does demand times price give us the most revenue?

When to use it

  • Pricing a product in a category buyers know.
  • Checking a planned price against demand.
  • Comparing demand across segments.

When to skip it

  • New categories where buyers have no reference price.
  • Complex packages; use conjoint analysis to price features.

How to run it

  1. Describe the product clearly to buyers in the target segment.
  2. Ask purchase intent at a random first price, then adjust up or down.
  3. Calculate the share who would buy at each price.
  4. Multiply by price, find the peak, and sanity-check it.

Worked example: Tessera

Tessera is a fictional SaaS company whose software helps mid-size manufacturers plan and track equipment maintenance.

Common mistakes

  • Buyers say yes more easily in surveys than in real life.
  • Testing prices far from the real range.
  • Ignoring competitors’ prices beside yours.
