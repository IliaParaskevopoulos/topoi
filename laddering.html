Customer understanding

Laddering

An interview technique that climbs from product attributes to the consequences and values behind them.

Thomas Reynolds and Jonathan Gutman, 1988

  1. Start with what the buyer notices about the product. Ask: “Why is that important to you?”

    High protein, Swiss ingredients.

  2. Keep asking why. The answers move to what the attribute does for them.

    I stay full until evening, and I don’t have to cook after work.

  3. At the top are personal values, the end goals behind the choice.

    Looking after myself, even on busy days.

  4. Lead messages with the consequence, connect to the value, and prove with the attribute.

    “Real food that keeps you going, with 30 g of protein in every meal.”

The question it answers

Why does this attribute matter to buyers, and what do they ultimately want from it?

When to use it

  • Developing messaging for a product or segment.
  • Understanding why customers prefer you over a competitor.
  • Testing whether a feature matters at all.

When to skip it

  • Quick surveys. Laddering needs patient one-to-one conversation.

How to run it

  1. Ask buyers which attributes matter when choosing.
  2. For each, ask “why is that important?” until they reach a value.
  3. Map the ladders and look for common paths.
  4. Choose the strongest path for each segment and build messages on it.

Worked example: Orto Foods

Orto Foods is a fictional maker of chilled ready meals sold through Swiss supermarkets.

Attributes
High protein, Swiss ingredients.
Consequences
I stay full until evening, and I don’t have to cook after work.
Values
Looking after myself, even on busy days.
Use the ladder
“Real food that keeps you going, with 30 g of protein in every meal.”

Common mistakes

  • Stopping at the first answer.
  • Leading the buyer to the value you hoped for.
  • Messaging only with values, with no proof underneath.
