Market and competitive analysis

Strategy Canvas

Plot how you and your competitors invest across the factors buyers care about, then reshape your curve to stand apart.

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005

  1. Left to right: the factors the industry competes on. Bottom to top: how much each player offers.

    Price level, range, design advice, delivery speed, assembly, store experience.

  2. Draw the value curve of a typical competitor group.

    Discount chains: low price and wide range, little service.

  3. Draw another group. Similar curves show where the industry converges.

    Design stores: high price, beautiful stores, little help with delivery.

  4. Draw your own offer. A distinct shape is a sign of real differentiation.

    Nordhaus: mid price, strong advice, fast delivery and assembly.

  5. Decide what to drop, cut, increase or add, so your curve stands apart where buyers care.

    Reduce range; raise design advice; create free assembly for members.

The question it answers

Where do we look the same as competitors, and where could we break away?

When to use it

  • Your offer looks the same as everyone else’s.
  • Planning a new product or repositioning an old one.
  • Explaining your differentiation in one picture.

When to skip it

  • Without buyer input on which factors matter. The curve then reflects internal opinion.

How to run it

  1. List the factors the industry competes on and invests in.
  2. Score competitors and yourself from buyer interviews and research.
  3. Draw the curves and look for convergence.
  4. Use the four actions: eliminate, reduce, raise, create.
  5. Test the new curve with buyers.

Worked example: Nordhaus Home

Nordhaus Home is a fictional chain of 35 home-goods stores.

Common mistakes

  • Too many factors; six to ten is plenty.
  • Raising everything, which is a cost problem, not a strategy.
  • Factors described in internal terms rather than buyer terms.
