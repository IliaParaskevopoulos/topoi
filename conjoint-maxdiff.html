Pricing and packaging

Conjoint and MaxDiff

Make buyers trade features and prices off against each other, to learn what they truly value.

Conjoint: Paul Green and colleagues, 1970s. MaxDiff: Jordan Louviere, late 1980s

  1. Choose the attributes buyers trade off, each with realistic levels. Show buyers sets of options and ask which they would choose. Price is usually the most important attribute.

    Fee levels from 0.8% to 2.0%: 41% of what drives choice.

  2. The analysis gives each attribute an importance score, based on how much it changes choices.

    Same day, next day or three days: 34% of what drives choice.

  3. Lower-importance attributes still matter for some segments. MaxDiff, a simpler cousin, asks buyers to pick the most and least important item from short lists.

    Accounting software sync: 25% overall, but 40% for firms with more than ten staff.

  4. Use the scores to decide what goes in each package and what to charge for.

    Next-day payout in every plan; same-day payout and sync drive the upgrade.

The question it answers

Which features and price levels matter most when buyers have to choose?

When to use it

  • Designing packages and deciding which features to charge for.
  • Choosing between feature roadmaps.
  • Finding how much buyers will pay for an upgrade.

When to skip it

  • Very small samples or very new categories buyers cannot judge.

How to run it

  1. Pick four to six attributes with two to four realistic levels each.
  2. Build choice tasks with a survey tool that supports conjoint or MaxDiff.
  3. Field the survey to the target segment.
  4. Read importance and part-worth results, and simulate packages.

Worked example: Kontor

Kontor is a fictional fintech that lets small businesses get paid early on their invoices.

Common mistakes

  • Too many attributes, which tires respondents.
  • Unrealistic levels that distort the results.
  • Treating simulated market share as a forecast.
