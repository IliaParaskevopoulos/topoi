Pricing and packaging

Economic Value to the Customer

Start from the price of the buyer’s next best option, add the value of what makes you better, and price below the total.

John Forbis and Nitin Mehta, 1981

  1. Start with the price of the customer’s next best alternative.

    A competitor’s sand, delivered: EUR 80 per tonne.

  2. Add the money value of your positive differences, and subtract any negatives. The total is the economic value to this customer.

    Fewer rejected glass batches are worth EUR 35 per tonne: economic value EUR 115.

  3. Price between the reference value and the economic value, so both sides win.

    Halden prices at EUR 100: the customer keeps EUR 15 of the value.

  4. Use the calculation in sales, so buyers see the value, not just the price.

    A cost-per-good-tonne calculator in every first meeting.

The question it answers

What is our product really worth to this customer, compared with their next best option?

When to use it

  • B2B products with measurable economic benefits.
  • Defending a price premium.
  • Building value-selling tools.

When to skip it

  • When benefits cannot be quantified even roughly.

How to run it

  1. Identify the next best alternative for one segment.
  2. List every difference, positive and negative.
  3. Put a money value on each with customer data.
  4. Set the price between reference and economic value.
  5. Turn the calculation into a sales tool.

Worked example: Halden Minerals

Halden Minerals is a fictional supplier of high-purity silica sand to glass and solar-panel makers.

Common mistakes

  • Using your list price as the reference instead of the alternative’s.
  • Counting benefits the customer does not value.
  • Pricing at full economic value, which leaves no reason to switch.
