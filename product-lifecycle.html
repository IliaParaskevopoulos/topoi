Go-to-market and launch

Product Lifecycle

Products move through introduction, growth, maturity and decline, and each stage calls for different marketing.

Joel Dean, 1950; popularised by Theodore Levitt, 1965

  1. Track sales of a product or category from launch to withdrawal. The curve rises, flattens and eventually falls.

    Tessera places each of its products on one curve.

  2. Sales are low and costs high. The job is to educate the market, win first customers and prove the product works.

    Tessera Predict, the new failure-prediction module: pilots with a few plants and hands-on onboarding.

  3. Sales rise fast and competitors arrive. The job is to build preference, widen distribution and scale delivery.

    The technician mobile app: usage is rising fast and competitors are launching their own apps.

  4. Growth slows and competition shifts to price. The job is to defend share, differentiate on service and cut cost.

    Core maintenance scheduling: steady renewals, price pressure, competing on ease of use and support.

  5. Sales fall as needs change or better options appear. Harvest, reposition, or plan the withdrawal.

    The on-premise edition, which customers are leaving for the cloud version.

  6. Profit usually trails sales: negative at launch, highest in late growth or early maturity, then shrinking.

    Profit from core scheduling pays for the Predict launch.

The question it answers

Where is each product in its life, and what should marketing do differently because of it?

When to use it

  • Setting different goals for different products instead of one plan for all.
  • Reviewing a portfolio to make sure new products are coming through as old ones decline.
  • Explaining to leadership why a mature product gets less launch budget than a new one.

When to skip it

  • Predicting when a stage will end. The curve describes; it does not forecast.
  • Fashion or fad products whose curves look nothing like the classic shape.

How to run it

  1. Plot sales for each product over the last three to five years, alongside market growth.
  2. Place each product in a stage based on its sales trend and the state of competition.
  3. Write one goal per product that fits its stage: educate, grow share, defend, or harvest.
  4. Check the portfolio balance: enough products in introduction and growth to replace those in decline.
  5. Review once a year, because products move between stages.

Common mistakes

  • Treating decline as fate. A repositioning or a new use can restart growth.
  • Running growth-stage campaigns for a mature product, where the job is to defend and differentiate.
  • Confusing a category’s lifecycle with a single brand’s.
