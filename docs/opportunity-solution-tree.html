Planning and prioritization

Opportunity Solution Tree

A map from one business outcome to the customer needs behind it, the ideas that could meet them, and the tests that check those ideas.

Teresa Torres, Product Talk

  1. Start at the top with one outcome the team is asked to move. It should be measurable and within the team’s influence.

    Raise the share of businesses that finance a second invoice within 60 days from 26% to 35%.

  2. Map the customer needs, pains and desires you heard in interviews that, if addressed, would move the outcome. Phrase them in the customer’s words.

    “I’m not sure the first payout was worth the fee.” “I forget Kontor exists until cash gets tight.” “My accountant doesn’t know I use it.”

  3. Compare the opportunities and pick one to work on. Size, how often it comes up and fit with strategy all matter.

    Kontor picks “I forget Kontor exists until cash gets tight”, which came up in 9 of 12 interviews.

  4. Generate several solutions for the target opportunity, not just one. Comparing options leads to better choices.

    An alert when a large invoice is issued, a monthly cash-gap forecast, and suggested invoices to finance.

  5. For the most promising solution, find the riskiest assumptions and test them quickly before building anything.

    Before building alerts, Kontor shows a mock alert to 200 users and interviews 8 about how they plan cash.

The question it answers

Which customer need should we work on to move our outcome, and how do we know our idea will work?

When to use it

  • A team owns an outcome, such as retention, and has to choose what to work on.
  • Stakeholders push solutions before anyone has agreed on the customer need.
  • Product marketing and product want a shared view of what customers are telling them.

When to skip it

  • Without regular customer interviews. The opportunities would be guesses.
  • For a fixed project with a known scope, where there is nothing to discover.

How to run it

  1. Agree on one outcome with leadership and write it at the top.
  2. Interview customers every week and add the needs you hear as opportunities.
  3. Group similar opportunities and break big ones into smaller, more specific ones.
  4. Choose one target opportunity, then brainstorm at least three solutions for it.
  5. Test the riskiest assumption of the best solutions before you build or launch.

Worked example: Kontor

Common mistakes

  • Writing solutions in the opportunity row, such as “needs a dashboard”.
  • Going straight from the outcome to one favourite solution.
  • Letting the tree go stale. It is a working document, updated as you learn.
