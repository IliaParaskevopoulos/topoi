Product marketing frameworks, drawn one step at a time

Each framework comes with an animated diagram you can play, pause and step through, a worked B2B example, and a template you can copy.

Τόποι, topoi, means “places”. In Aristotle’s Rhetoric they are the places a speaker goes to find an argument. This library is the same idea for product marketers.

Customer understanding

Who buys, what they are trying to get done, and what stands in their way.

Jobs-to-be-Done

Clayton Christensen and colleagues, 2003; job stories from Intercom, around 2013

Customers do not buy products; they “hire” them to make progress in a specific situation.

Outcome-Driven Innovation

Tony Ulwick, Strategyn, 1990s

Break a customer’s job into measurable outcomes, then find the ones that matter most and are served worst.

Forces of Progress

Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, early 2010s

Four forces decide whether a customer switches: two push towards change, two hold them back.

Value Proposition Canvas

Alexander Osterwalder and colleagues, Strategyzer, 2014

Two shapes side by side: what a customer is trying to get done, and how your product helps. Fit is where they match.

5 Rings of Buying Insight

Adele Revella, Buyer Persona Institute, 2015

A buyer persona built from what buyers actually say about their decision, in five parts.

Ideal Customer Profile

Common B2B practice; no single author

A description of the companies that get the most value from you and are most valuable to you.

Buying Committee Map

Common B2B sales and marketing practice

Map the people behind a B2B decision, what each cares about, and where each stands.

Kano Model

Noriaki Kano and colleagues, 1984

Not every feature affects satisfaction the same way. Some are expected, some scale with effort, and some delight.

Customer Journey Map

Service design and marketing practice; no single author

Map the stages a customer goes through, and how they feel at each, to find where to help.

Voice of the Customer

Abbie Griffin and John Hauser, 1993

A steady programme that collects what customers say, turns it into themes, and gets it acted on.

Win/Loss Analysis

Standard product marketing practice; no single author

Ask buyers why they chose you or someone else, and use the patterns to improve how you sell.

Laddering

Thomas Reynolds and Jonathan Gutman, 1988

An interview technique that climbs from product attributes to the consequences and values behind them.

Sean Ellis Test

Sean Ellis, around 2009

One survey question that shows whether a product has product-market fit: how would you feel if you could no longer use it?

Net Promoter Score

Fred Reichheld, Bain and Company, 2003

One question about how likely customers are to recommend you, turned into a score you can track.

Market and competitive analysis

Where to compete, how attractive the market is, and how you compare.

SWOT and TOWS

SWOT: 1960s, origin disputed. TOWS: Heinz Weihrich, 1982

Strengths and weaknesses inside the company, opportunities and threats outside it, turned into actions.

PESTLE

Francis Aguilar, 1967 (as ETPS); later extended to PESTLE

Scan six kinds of outside change that could open or close opportunities for your product.

Porter’s Five Forces

Michael Porter, 1979

Five pressures that decide how much profit an industry leaves for the companies in it.

Ansoff Matrix

Igor Ansoff, 1957

Four ways to grow, sorted by how far each one takes you from what you already sell and who you already sell to.

BCG Growth-Share Matrix

Bruce Henderson, Boston Consulting Group, 1970

Sort products by market growth and market share, to decide where to invest and where to take cash out.

Strategy Canvas

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne, Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005

Plot how you and your competitors invest across the factors buyers care about, then reshape your curve to stand apart.

TAM, SAM, SOM

Common market-sizing practice; no single author

Three nested market sizes: everyone who could ever buy, those you can actually serve, and the share you can realistically win.

Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning

Wendell Smith, 1956; popularised by Philip Kotler

Divide the market into groups, choose the ones to serve, then decide how you want them to see you.

Perceptual Map

Marketing research practice since the 1960s

Place competitors on the two dimensions buyers care about most, to see where you stand and where there is room.

Competitive Intelligence Cycle

Adapted from the intelligence cycle by SCIP practitioners

A repeating loop that turns questions about competitors into information people use to decide.

Positioning

The context that makes your product’s value obvious to the right buyer.

Obviously Awesome

April Dunford, 2019

A positioning method that starts from what customers would do without you, then works out what makes you different and who cares most.

Positioning Statement

Geoffrey Moore, 1991

One structured sentence that says who the product is for, what it is, why it matters and how it differs.

Positioning (Ries and Trout)

Al Ries and Jack Trout, 1972; book 1981

Positioning happens in the buyer’s mind: find your place on the mental ladder for your category, and own one idea.

Onlyness Statement

Marty Neumeier, Zag, 2006

Complete the sentence “we are the only…”. If you cannot, you are not different enough yet.

Category Design

Al Ramadan, Dave Peterson, Christopher Lochhead and Kevin Maney, 2016

Instead of competing in an existing category, define a new one around a problem, and lead it.

Whole Product

Theodore Levitt, 1980; adapted by Geoffrey Moore, 1991

Customers buy more than the core product: everything around it that makes it work for them.

Crossing the Chasm

Geoffrey Moore, 1991, building on Everett Rogers

New technology spreads through groups of buyers in order, and the hardest jump is from early enthusiasts to the pragmatic mainstream.

Brand Architecture

David Aaker and Erich Joachimsthaler, 2000

Decide how your company brand and product names relate, from one master brand to separate brands.

Messaging and storytelling

Turning positioning into words that sales, web and campaigns can use.

Messaging House

Practitioner tool, widely used in B2B marketing and PR

One umbrella message, held up by three pillars with proof, standing on what makes you credible.

Message Hierarchy

Common product marketing practice; no single author

One value proposition at the top, a few key messages under it, and supporting points and proof below.

Strategic Narrative

Andy Raskin, 2016

A sales story that starts with a big change in the world, not with your product.

Sales Pitch (Dunford)

April Dunford, 2023

A pitch built on your positioning: start with an insight about the market, then show why you are the best answer.

StoryBrand

Donald Miller, 2017

Tell your marketing as a story where the customer is the hero and your company is the guide.

B2B Elements of Value

Eric Almquist, Jamie Cleghorn and Lori Sherer, Bain and Company, 2018

A pyramid of the kinds of value B2B buyers look for, from basics to personal and inspirational value.

Features, Advantages, Benefits

Classic sales-training technique; no single author

Turn what a product has into what it does, and then into what the customer gains.

Benefit Ladder

Common messaging practice, rooted in means-end research

Climb from a feature to its functional benefit to its emotional benefit, then choose the rung to lead with.

Value Proposition Formula

Steve Blank’s “We help X do Y by doing Z”

One sentence: who you help, what you help them do, how, and compared with what.

Why Change, Why Now, Why Us

Erik Peterson and Tim Riesterer, Corporate Visions, 2011

Most deals are lost to “no decision”. Answer why the buyer should change, why now, and why you.

Pricing and packaging

What to charge, how to package it, and what buyers will accept.

Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter

Peter van Westendorp, 1976

Four questions about price that show the range buyers find acceptable, and where in that range to aim.

Gabor-Granger

André Gabor and Clive Granger, 1960s

Ask buyers whether they would buy at a series of prices, then find the price that earns the most revenue.

Conjoint and MaxDiff

Conjoint: Paul Green and colleagues, 1970s. MaxDiff: Jordan Louviere, late 1980s

Make buyers trade features and prices off against each other, to learn what they truly value.

Economic Value to the Customer

John Forbis and Nitin Mehta, 1981

Start from the price of the buyer’s next best option, add the value of what makes you better, and price below the total.

Value-Based Pricing

Pricing practice described by Thomas Nagle and colleagues

Set prices from the value customers get, not from your costs or competitors’ prices alone.

Leaders, Fillers, Killers

Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke, Simon-Kucher, 2016

Sort features into those that drive the purchase, those that fill out a package, and those that put buyers off.

Good, Better, Best

Long-standing retail practice; now standard in software pricing

Three versions at three prices, so each segment finds its fit and most choose the middle.

Value Metric

Common software pricing practice; no single author

Choose the unit you charge for so that price grows with the value the customer gets.

Pricing Models

Common pricing practice; no single author

The main ways to structure what customers pay, from a single flat fee to hybrids.

Price Waterfall

Michael Marn and Robert Rosiello, McKinsey, 1992

Follow the price from list to what you actually keep, to find where money leaks away.

Go-to-market and launch

Getting a product to market: motions, plans and launch mechanics.

Go-to-Market Canvas

Common go-to-market planning practice; no single author

One page that answers the core go-to-market questions: who, what problem, what value, what price, which channels, how you will measure.

Launch Tiers

Common product marketing practice; no single author

Not every release deserves a big launch. Sort releases into tiers, each with its own level of effort.

T-minus Launch Plan

Common launch-management practice; no single author

Count back from launch day, with phases and milestones, so everything is ready on time.

Beachhead Market

Bill Aulet, Disciplined Entrepreneurship, 2013

Choose one small market to win completely first, then use it as a base to expand.

Bowling Alley

Geoffrey Moore, Inside the Tornado, 1995

After the first segment, expand to neighbouring segments one by one, like knocking down bowling pins.

Marketing Mix: 4Ps and 7Ps

E. Jerome McCarthy, 1960 (4Ps); Bernard Booms and Mary Jo Bitner, 1981 (7Ps)

The decisions that bring a product to market: product, price, place and promotion, plus three more for services.

SAVE

Richard Ettenson, Eduardo Conrado and Jonathan Knowles, 2013

A B2B update to the 4Ps: solution instead of product, access instead of place, value instead of price, education instead of promotion.

Go-to-Market Motions

Common software go-to-market practice; no single author

Choose how customers first buy: through the product itself, through salespeople, or a mix, depending on deal size.

Account-Based Marketing Tiers

ITSMA, early 2000s

Match marketing effort to account value: broad programmes for many, tailored ones for a few, and fully custom work for the most important.

Product Lifecycle

Joel Dean, 1950; popularised by Theodore Levitt, 1965

Products move through introduction, growth, maturity and decline, and each stage calls for different marketing.

Working Backwards PR/FAQ

Amazon; described by Colin Bryar and Bill Carr, 2021

Write the launch press release and FAQ before building anything, to test whether the idea is worth doing.

PESO Model

Gini Dietrich, 2014

Four kinds of media, paid, earned, shared and owned, that work best when one piece of work feeds all of them.

Sales enablement

Helping sales qualify, sell value and handle objections.

Battlecard

Standard product marketing practice; no single author

A one-page guide that helps sellers win against a specific competitor.

MEDDIC

Jack Napoli and Dick Dunkel, PTC, 1996

Six things you must know to qualify a complex B2B deal, and to tell a real opportunity from hope.

BANT

IBM, 1950s–60s

Four quick checks to qualify a lead: budget, authority, need and timing.

SPIN Selling

Neil Rackham, 1988

Four kinds of question that move a buyer from describing their situation to stating the value of a solution.

Challenger Sale

Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson, CEB, 2011

Win by teaching buyers something new about their business, then showing how you solve it.

Command of the Message

Force Management, 2000s

A value framework that ties buyers’ problems to business outcomes, the capabilities needed, and your proof.

Solution Selling

Michael Bosworth, 1995

Sell by diagnosing the buyer’s pain and building a shared picture of the solution, not by pitching the product.

Value Selling

ValueSelling Associates, 1990s

A simple formula: vision times value times power equals a plan to a decision.

B2B Buying Jobs

Gartner, 2019

B2B buyers do not move in a straight line. They work through six jobs, often going back and forth.

LAER

Carew International

Handle objections in four steps: listen, acknowledge, explore, respond.

Content-to-Stage Map

Common product marketing practice; no single author

Map every piece of content to the stage where buyers and customers need it, and find the gaps.

Kirkpatrick Model

Donald Kirkpatrick, 1959

Measure sales training at four levels: reaction, learning, behaviour and business results.

Adoption and growth

Measuring how customers move from first contact to loyal, growing accounts.

AARRR Pirate Metrics

Dave McClure, 2007

Five stages that turn the customer journey into numbers, so you can see where you lose people.

Flywheel

HubSpot, 2018, building on Jim Collins’s flywheel idea

A circle instead of a funnel: happy customers feed growth, so energy you put in keeps the wheel turning.

Bow-Tie Model

Winning by Design (Jacco van der Kooij), 2010s

A funnel that does not stop at the sale: after the commitment, the customer journey widens again through onboarding, impact and growth.

TOFU, MOFU, BOFU

Common marketing practice; no single author

Plan content for the top, middle and bottom of the funnel, so buyers find help at every stage.
