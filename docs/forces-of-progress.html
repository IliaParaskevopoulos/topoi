Customer understanding

Forces of Progress

Four forces decide whether a customer switches: two push towards change, two hold them back.

Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, early 2010s

  1. Top row: forces about the new solution. Bottom row: forces about the current way. Left: forces that promote a switch. Right: forces that block it.

    Kontor interviews business owners who signed up in the last three months.

  2. Problems with the current way that push the customer to look for something new.

    Customers paying in 60 to 90 days leave payroll tight every month.

  3. What attracts them to the new way: the better life they imagine.

    Cash within 24 hours, without a meeting at the bank.

  4. Worries about the new solution: will it work, what will it cost, what will others think.

    “Will my customers find out? Is the fee worth it?”

  5. The comfort of the current way, even when it is not good.

    “We have always used the bank overdraft.”

  6. A switch happens when push and pull outweigh anxiety and habit. Reducing anxiety often helps more than adding pull.

    Kontor’s sign-up page answers anxiety first: customers are never contacted, and the fee is shown up front.

The question it answers

What makes customers switch to us, and what stops them even when they want to?

When to use it

  • Conversion is low even though interest is high.
  • Writing onboarding, FAQ and objection-handling content.
  • Understanding why customers leave a competitor for you.

When to skip it

  • Without real switching stories. Hypothetical answers hide the forces.

How to run it

  1. Interview recent switchers about the timeline of their decision, from first thought to purchase.
  2. Note every push, pull, anxiety and habit in their own words.
  3. Group the forces across interviews and rank them by frequency.
  4. Strengthen push and pull in messaging; remove anxieties with proof, guarantees and trials.

Worked example: Kontor

Kontor is a fictional fintech that lets small businesses get paid early on their invoices.

Common mistakes

  • Only adding features (pull) while anxieties stay unanswered.
  • Interviewing people who never seriously considered switching.
  • Ignoring habit, which often decides the outcome.
