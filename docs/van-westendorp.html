Pricing and packaging

Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter

Four questions about price that show the range buyers find acceptable, and where in that range to aim.

Peter van Westendorp, 1976

  1. Show buyers the product and ask: at what price is it so cheap you would doubt the quality? A bargain? Getting expensive, but still worth considering? Too expensive to consider?

    Orto asks 400 shoppers about a new 400 g chilled lasagne.

  2. For each price, plot the share of buyers who find it too cheap. The curve falls as price rises.

    Below about CHF 5.60, most shoppers start to doubt the quality.

  3. Plot the share who see each price as a bargain. This curve also falls as price rises.

    At about CHF 7.60, half the shoppers still see it as a bargain.

  4. Plot the share who find each price expensive but would still consider buying. This curve rises with price.

    At CHF 9, half the shoppers find it getting expensive.

  5. Plot the share who would not consider buying at each price.

    Above CHF 11, most shoppers would not consider it.

  6. The range runs from where “too cheap” crosses “getting expensive” to where “too expensive” crosses “bargain”. Some practitioners use inverted “not a bargain” and “not expensive” curves instead; the idea is the same.

    Orto’s acceptable range is CHF 7.00 to CHF 9.20.

  7. Where “too cheap” meets “too expensive” is the optimal price point: the fewest buyers reject the price. Where “bargain” meets “getting expensive” is the indifference point.

    Optimal at CHF 7.70, indifference at CHF 8.30. Orto launches at CHF 7.90, between the two, and keeps promotions above CHF 7.00.

The question it answers

What price range will buyers accept for this product, and where in that range should we set the price?

When to use it

  • Pricing a new product, pack size or variant in a category where buyers know roughly what things cost.
  • Checking whether a planned price sits inside what buyers will accept.
  • Getting a quick, low-cost read before a more detailed study.

When to skip it

  • Brand-new categories where buyers have no reference price. Answers will be guesses.
  • Large B2B deals with negotiated, value-based prices.
  • Predicting volume or revenue. Combine it with Gabor-Granger or conjoint analysis for that.

How to run it

  1. Recruit buyers from the target segment and show them a realistic description of the product.
  2. Ask the four questions in order: too expensive, expensive, bargain, too cheap. Let them answer in open price amounts.
  3. For each price point, calculate the cumulative share for each question.
  4. Plot the four curves and find the intersections.
  5. Set the price inside the acceptable range, using the optimal and indifference points and your costs as guides.

Common mistakes

  • Asking people who do not buy in the category. Their answers are not anchored in reality.
  • Reading the optimal price point as the profit-maximising price. It is the price fewest people reject, nothing more.
  • Ignoring the competitive shelf. Buyers will compare your price with the products next to it.
