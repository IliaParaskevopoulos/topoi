Pricing and packaging

Good, Better, Best

Three versions at three prices, so each segment finds its fit and most choose the middle.

Long-standing retail practice; now standard in software pricing

  1. The entry tier: enough to solve the core need, at a low barrier. It brings in price-sensitive buyers.

    Pay as you go: 1.9% fee per invoice, no monthly cost.

  2. The tier you want most customers to choose: the best value for the main segment.

    CHF 49 a month: 1.2% fee, automatic sync, two users.

  3. The premium tier for buyers who need more. It also makes the middle tier look reasonable.

    CHF 149 a month: 0.8% fee, a credit line, a named contact.

  4. Set the differences so the middle tier is the obvious choice for your main segment. Each step up should add value one group clearly wants.

    Businesses that finance more than about CHF 7,000 a month pay less on the CHF 49 plan, so most choose it.

The question it answers

How do we package our offer so different customers pay for the value they need?

When to use it

  • Customers differ clearly in how much they use or value the product.
  • Launching a new pricing model.
  • Too many buyers choose the cheapest option, or none.

When to skip it

  • Products with one clear use and one buyer type.
  • Large negotiated deals priced case by case.

How to run it

  1. Segment customers by the value they get, not by company size alone.
  2. Choose a value metric that grows with value, such as invoices or users.
  3. Put each feature in the lowest tier where its main segment needs it.
  4. Price the tiers with research such as Van Westendorp or conjoint.
  5. Watch the tier mix after launch and adjust the fences.

Worked example: Kontor

Kontor is a fictional fintech that lets small businesses get paid early on their invoices.

Common mistakes

  • A good tier so complete that nobody upgrades.
  • Tiers that differ only in quantity, with no reason to step up.
  • Too many tiers, which slows the decision.
