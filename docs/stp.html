Market and competitive analysis

Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning

Divide the market into groups, choose the ones to serve, then decide how you want them to see you.

Wendell Smith, 1956; popularised by Philip Kotler

  1. Divide the market into groups with different needs or behaviours. Needs-based segments are more useful than demographics alone.

    Busy working parents, fitness-focused singles, and older people living alone.

  2. Score each segment on size, growth, fit and competition, and choose where to focus.

    Fitness-focused singles: growing, willing to pay more, and few strong brands serve them.

  3. Decide what you want the chosen segment to think of you, compared with the alternatives.

    The high-protein ready meal that tastes home-cooked.

The question it answers

Which customers should we serve, and how should we position ourselves for them?

When to use it

  • Entering a market or launching a product.
  • Marketing is spread too thin across everyone.
  • Before writing positioning and messaging.

When to skip it

  • When segments are already clear and agreed. Go straight to positioning.

How to run it

  1. Segment by needs and behaviour, then describe each segment with data you can act on.
  2. Score segments on attractiveness and on your ability to win.
  3. Choose one to three segments.
  4. Write a positioning for each and check it against competitors.

Worked example: Orto Foods

Orto Foods is a fictional maker of chilled ready meals sold through Swiss supermarkets.

Segment
Busy working parents, fitness-focused singles, and older people living alone.
Target
Fitness-focused singles: growing, willing to pay more, and few strong brands serve them.
Position
The high-protein ready meal that tastes home-cooked.

Common mistakes

  • Segments you cannot reach or measure.
  • Targeting every segment “a little”.
  • Positioning written before choosing a target.
