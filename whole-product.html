Positioning

Whole Product

Customers buy more than the core product: everything around it that makes it work for them.

Theodore Levitt, 1980; adapted by Geoffrey Moore, 1991

  1. The core thing you ship. On its own, it rarely meets the whole need.

    The rapid test reader and its test cartridges.

  2. What customers assume comes with it. Missing any of these blocks the sale.

    Validated accuracy, staff training and a service contract.

  3. What goes beyond expectations and helps the customer get the most out of it.

    Results sent straight to the patient record, and billing codes prepared.

  4. What the product could become as the market grows, with partners and new features.

    A network of practices sharing anonymised data on local infections.

  5. Mainstream buyers want the whole product. Build or partner for it one segment at a time, starting with your beachhead.

    Lumora completes the whole product for paediatric practices first.

The question it answers

What does the customer need around our core product before they can get the full value from it?

When to use it

  • Moving from early adopters to mainstream customers.
  • Sales stall even though the core product works.
  • Planning partnerships and services around a product.

When to skip it

  • Simple, self-contained products with nothing around them.
  • As a feature wish list. The question is what this segment needs to buy.

How to run it

  1. Write the generic product in one line.
  2. Ask sales and service what customers assume comes with it, and what blocks deals.
  3. List what would help customers get more value: integrations, services, content.
  4. Decide for each item: build, partner or bundle.
  5. Complete the whole product for one segment before widening.

Worked example: Lumora Diagnostics

Lumora Diagnostics is a fictional company that makes a rapid blood-test reader for GP clinics.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the core product as the whole offer.
  • Trying to complete the whole product for every segment at once.
  • Forgetting partners, who often supply the missing pieces.
