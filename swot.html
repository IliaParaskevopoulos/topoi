Market and competitive analysis

SWOT and TOWS

Strengths and weaknesses inside the company, opportunities and threats outside it, turned into actions.

SWOT: 1960s, origin disputed. TOWS: Heinz Weihrich, 1982

  1. Top row: inside the company. Bottom row: outside it. Left column: helpful. Right column: harmful.

    Orto Foods runs a SWOT before its annual plan.

  2. Internal things that help you win: capabilities, assets, reputation, relationships.

    Strong supermarket listings and a trusted Swiss-made brand.

  3. Internal things that hold you back. Be specific, and include what customers complain about.

    Short shelf life raises waste and logistics costs.

  4. External changes you could use: trends, new segments, regulation, competitor moves.

    Rising demand for high-protein and plant-based meals.

  5. External changes that could hurt you: new competitors, substitutes, cost increases, rules.

    Supermarkets’ own-label ready meals at lower prices.

  6. A SWOT on its own decides nothing. Pair the boxes (the TOWS step): use strengths for opportunities, fix weaknesses that threats expose.

    Orto uses its brand to lead in high-protein meals, and cuts waste to answer own-label prices.

The question it answers

What do we have going for us, what holds us back, and what is changing around us?

When to use it

  • At the start of an annual plan or strategy review.
  • Before entering a new market, to get a shared view quickly.
  • Briefing a new team or agency.

When to skip it

  • As the whole strategy. It describes the situation; it does not choose.
  • When the team cannot be honest about weaknesses. The result will flatter you.

How to run it

  1. Agree the scope: the whole company, one product, or one market.
  2. Collect input from sales, product, operations and customers, not only marketing.
  3. Keep each box to five points or fewer, each specific enough to act on.
  4. Pair the boxes into TOWS moves: strengths with opportunities, weaknesses with threats.
  5. Turn the two or three strongest moves into objectives with owners.

Worked example: Orto Foods

Orto Foods is a fictional maker of chilled ready meals sold through Swiss supermarkets.

Common mistakes

  • Vague points such as “good people” or “competition”.
  • Listing internal plans as opportunities. Opportunities are outside the company.
  • Stopping at the grid without deciding anything.
