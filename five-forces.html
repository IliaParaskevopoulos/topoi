Market and competitive analysis

Porter’s Five Forces

Five pressures that decide how much profit an industry leaves for the companies in it.

Michael Porter, 1979

  1. Start in the middle: how hard do existing competitors fight? Many similar players, slow growth and high fixed costs make rivalry fierce.

    A handful of regional quarries compete on purity and on the cost of freight to the customer’s plant.

  2. How easily can a new player join? High capital needs, regulation, scale advantages and strong brands keep newcomers out.

    A new quarry needs permits that take five to ten years and heavy investment. Few newcomers try.

  3. How much can your suppliers raise prices or cut quality? Supplier power is high when there are few of them or switching is hard.

    Energy and rail freight are a large share of cost, and there are only a few providers.

  4. How much can your customers push prices down? Buyer power is high when a few customers buy most of the volume or can switch easily.

    A few large glassmakers buy most of the volume and negotiate hard every year.

  5. What else could do the same job for the customer, from a different industry or with a different approach?

    Recycled glass partly replaces new sand in container glass, but not in solar-grade glass.

  6. Rate each force high, medium or low. The more forces are high, the less profit the industry leaves for its players. Then ask which forces you can change.

    Buyer power is the biggest threat, so Halden shifts its mix towards solar makers, where purity matters more than price.

The question it answers

How attractive is this market, and which pressures will squeeze our margins?

When to use it

  • Deciding whether to enter a market or segment.
  • Explaining to leadership why margins are under pressure.
  • Preparing a strategy that changes the balance of power, for example by reducing buyer concentration.

When to skip it

  • Comparing your product with one competitor’s. Use a battlecard or a feature comparison.
  • Fast-changing markets where the boundaries of the industry are unclear. Define the industry first, or the analysis drifts.

How to run it

  1. Define the industry precisely: which product, which customers, which region.
  2. For each force, list the specific factors that make it strong or weak.
  3. Rate each force high, medium or low, and write down why.
  4. Look at where the profit goes: to you, your suppliers, your buyers, or nobody.
  5. Choose one or two forces you can change, and build the plan around them.

Worked example: Halden Minerals

Halden Minerals is a fictional supplier of high-purity silica sand to glass and solar-panel makers.

Rivalry among competitors
A handful of regional quarries compete on purity and on the cost of freight to the customer’s plant.
Threat of new entrants
A new quarry needs permits that take five to ten years and heavy investment. Few newcomers try.
Power of suppliers
Energy and rail freight are a large share of cost, and there are only a few providers.
Power of buyers
A few large glassmakers buy most of the volume and negotiate hard every year.
Threat of substitutes
Recycled glass partly replaces new sand in container glass, but not in solar-grade glass.
Rate each force
Buyer power is the biggest threat, so Halden shifts its mix towards solar makers, where purity matters more than price.

Common mistakes

  • Defining the industry too broadly, such as “minerals” instead of “high-purity silica for glass”.
  • Listing factors without rating them, which leaves no conclusion.
  • Treating the result as fixed. Regulation, technology and consolidation change the forces over time.
