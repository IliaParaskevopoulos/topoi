Customer understanding

Outcome-Driven Innovation

Break a customer’s job into measurable outcomes, then find the ones that matter most and are served worst.

Tony Ulwick, Strategyn, 1990s

  1. Pick one job the customer is trying to get done and set up a grid: satisfaction left to right, importance bottom to top.

    The job: keep production equipment running.

  2. Write outcome statements in a fixed form (“minimise the time it takes to…”) and have customers rate each for importance and satisfaction. The line marks where the two are equal.

    Tessera writes 60 outcome statements and surveys 180 maintenance managers.

  3. High importance, low satisfaction. These are the best opportunities to lead with.

    “Minimise the time to learn a machine is about to fail”: importance 9.1, satisfaction 3.2.

  4. Satisfaction is higher than importance. Competing here wastes effort; it can even be simplified.

    “Minimise the effort to schedule routine checks”: importance 6.0, satisfaction 8.5.

  5. Opportunity score = importance + the gap between importance and satisfaction (if positive). Scores above 10 are strong opportunities.

    Early failure warning scores 15.0, so it leads Tessera’s roadmap and messaging.

The question it answers

Which customer outcomes are important but poorly served today, so we can lead with them?

When to use it

  • Deciding which needs to build and position around.
  • Finding segments with different unmet needs.
  • Backing messaging choices with numbers, not opinion.

When to skip it

  • Very early markets where customers cannot yet describe the job.
  • When you cannot survey enough customers for reliable ratings.

How to run it

  1. Define the job in the customer’s words, without your product in it.
  2. Interview customers to map the job steps and collect outcome statements.
  3. Survey customers on importance and satisfaction for each outcome.
  4. Calculate opportunity scores and plot the landscape.
  5. Lead product and messaging with the highest-scoring outcomes.

Common mistakes

  • Outcome statements that describe solutions (“have a dashboard”).
  • Averaging across segments that have different needs.
  • Treating overserved outcomes as strengths to advertise.
