Market and competitive analysis

Perceptual Map

Place competitors on the two dimensions buyers care about most, to see where you stand and where there is room.

Marketing research practice since the 1960s

  1. Pick the two dimensions buyers use most when comparing. Ask them; do not guess.

    Speed to cash, and paperwork and effort.

  2. Position each competitor or alternative based on buyer perception, not your opinion.

    Bank factoring: slow, heavy paperwork. Invoice marketplaces: medium on both.

  3. Put yourself where buyers see you, which may differ from where you want to be.

    Kontor: fast, with little effort.

  4. Look for areas buyers value that nobody owns, and check that real demand exists there.

    Fast and low-effort is open; Kontor’s messaging claims it outright.

The question it answers

How do buyers see us compared with the alternatives, and where is there open space?

When to use it

  • Explaining differentiation to leadership, sales or investors.
  • Looking for an underserved position.
  • Tracking how perception changes after repositioning.

When to skip it

  • When buyers decide on many factors at once; use a strategy canvas instead.

How to run it

  1. Ask buyers which dimensions matter most when they compare.
  2. Rate each option on those dimensions from buyer research.
  3. Plot and discuss what the picture shows.
  4. Check that the open space is something buyers actually want.

Worked example: Kontor

Kontor is a fictional fintech that lets small businesses get paid early on their invoices.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing dimensions that make you look good.
  • Using your own view of competitors instead of buyers’.
  • Assuming every empty space is an opportunity.
